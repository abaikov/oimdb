OIMDB — THE PRICE OF EVERY OPERATION AGAINST THE PRIMITIVE IT REPLACES

Each row is one call this library offers, timed against the plain JavaScript a person would have
written instead of it. Not against another library — against the floor. The ratio is ours over
the floor, and it is a price tag rather than a ranking.

WHERE THE FLOOR AND OUR CALL ARE NOT DOING THE SAME WORK

There is no pair in this suite whose two sides do identical work, and a floor that does less is a
lie in our favour. Two of these are structural and apply to most rows: a Map-keyed floor pays a
hash where our identity path does not, which flatters us; and our write additionally marks a channel
and may route to indexes the floor knows nothing about, which does not. Each row says its own.

A THIRD ONE BELONGS TO THE INSTRUMENT AND APPLIES TO EVERY ROW. Both sides are driven by a loop —
an increment, a bounds check, an index wrap and one store into a sink — and that loop is charged
to the numerator and the denominator alike. Adding the same constant to both pulls a ratio toward
1.00, and it pulls hardest where the operation is cheapest, so the sub-nanosecond rows are the ones
this understates most. The wrap is a mask rather than a division for exactly that reason. Read
`find.vs-map-get` as the calibration: it is one call and one Map.get on our side against one Map.get
on the floor, so whatever it reports above 1.00 is mostly this.

new-db.vs-hand-written-frame-state   [per declaration]   205x
  ours   new OIMDB()
  floor  the object a person keeps instead: a frame number and a queue
  NOT A LIKE-FOR-LIKE FLOOR. Nobody hand-writes a database; the nearest thing is the
  bookkeeping a person keeps beside their `Map`, which is a frame counter and a list of what
  to repaint. Ours constructs a subscriber store, an effect scheduler, two delivery lists, a
  frame record, a release set and the bound callbacks that hold them together. Direction:
  inflates our ratio without limit, and it is paid ONCE per application. It is on the table
  because a price nobody prints is a price nobody believes, not because the multiple means
  anything.
  measured  42,182 ops/pass ours, 8,914,380 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 20 planned, 20/20 reached; spread 5.0%/5.9%; load 2.40 → 2.40 on 8 cores

new-collection.vs-new-map   [per declaration]   5.56x
  ours   new OIMCollectionByKey(db, row => row.id)
  floor  new Map()
  A population against the container that would have held it. Ours also builds the identity
  store, the presence channel, the empty index list, the free-ordinal list, the dependents map
  and the projection list, and it keeps the key selector — which is the closure a caller
  passes and which the floor does not need because the caller writes `row.id` at every call
  site instead. Direction: inflates our ratio; paid once per population.
  measured  266,696 ops/pass ours, 2,097,152 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 33 planned, 33/33 reached; spread 25.3%/13.1%; load 2.36 → 2.36 on 8 cores

addIndex.over-a-loaded-population-vs-hand-built-map   [per declaration]   4.43x
  ours   addIndex over a population already holding a thousand rows, then removeIndex
  floor  build a Map of Sets from the same thousand rows, then drop it
  BOTH SIDES BUILD AND THEN GIVE BACK, which is what makes them comparable at all: declaring
  an index on a live population widens every existing row and files it, so the operation is
  O(rows) exactly as building the map is, and a body that only ever added would accumulate
  indexes and price a growing population instead. Ours also widens each row's per-index cell
  array, registers the index's maintenance node in the effect graph, and mints a hold-counted
  bucket identity per group where the floor mints a bare `Set`. The teardown differs in kind
  and both are inside the clock: ours unlinks every row and frees the ordinal, the floor drops
  a reference and lets the collector deal with it later — so part of the floor's teardown is
  paid outside this number and part of ours is inside it. Direction: inflates our ratio.
  measured  1,024 ops/pass ours, 1,024 floor; 5 samples; warm-up 7 planned, 7/7 reached; spread 3.2%/10.2%; load 2.36 → 2.36 on 8 cores

THE CONTROLS, IN FULL

control-resolution-2x   1.93x
  ours   two units of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  NOT A PRICE — an instrument check, and it is printed first because nothing below it means
  anything until it passes. The true ratio is a little UNDER 2: both sides pay the driving
  loop once, so the exact expectation is (2u + L) / (u + L) with L the loop and u one unit,
  which at sixteen dependent steps per unit puts it around 1.9. A reading below 1.5 means this
  process could not resolve a deliberate doubling, and the runner voids the report rather than
  printing prices under it.

control-scale-same-body   1.00x
  ours   one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  the same body again, over its own copy of the same state
  NOT A PRICE — the noise floor. The two sides are identical code over identical,
  separately-allocated state, so the true ratio is 1.00 exactly and every departure from it
  printed here was produced by this machine at this moment. The rule that follows is the one
  the whole table is read under: NO RATIO CLOSER TO 1.00 THAN THIS GAP IS A READING. It also
  bounds the per-row noise figure, which is the same idea measured again inside every pair —
  each pair times its floor twice, once before our side and once after, and prints how far the
  two disagreed.
