OIMDB — THE PRICE OF EVERY OPERATION AGAINST THE PRIMITIVE IT REPLACES

Each row is one call this library offers, timed against the plain JavaScript a person would have
written instead of it. Not against another library — against the floor. The ratio is ours over
the floor, and it is a price tag rather than a ranking.

WHERE THE FLOOR AND OUR CALL ARE NOT DOING THE SAME WORK

There is no pair in this suite whose two sides do identical work, and a floor that does less is a
lie in our favour. Two of these are structural and apply to most rows: a Map-keyed floor pays a
hash where our identity path does not, which flatters us; and our write additionally marks a channel
and may route to indexes the floor knows nothing about, which does not. Each row says its own.

A THIRD ONE BELONGS TO THE INSTRUMENT AND APPLIES TO EVERY ROW. Both sides are driven by a loop —
an increment, a bounds check, an index wrap and one store into a sink — and that loop is charged
to the numerator and the denominator alike. Adding the same constant to both pulls a ratio toward
1.00, and it pulls hardest where the operation is cheapest, so the sub-nanosecond rows are the ones
this understates most. The wrap is a mask rather than a division for exactly that reason. Read
`find.vs-map-get` as the calibration: it is one call and one Map.get on our side against one Map.get
on the floor, so whatever it reports above 1.00 is mostly this.

groups.resolve-vs-map-get   [per relationship]   29.0x
  ours   groupBy(collection, ['group'])('group-7') — resolve a group to a live handle
  floor  byGroup.get('group-7')
  THE MOST UNEQUAL PAIR IN THIS SUITE, and it is on the table because the engine's own
  documentation says "resolved ONCE — hold it", so the row is what IGNORING that advice costs.
  Ours resolves the bucket, takes a hold on it so the frame sweep cannot drop it underneath
  the caller, and builds an observable — a getter closure, a subscribe closure, a detach
  closure and the object they hang on. Five allocations against a hash. The floor hands back a
  container that can be iterated and cannot be subscribed to, cannot be handed to
  `db.computed`, and will be the WRONG container the moment somebody rebuilds it. Direction:
  inflates our ratio enormously, and it is a real number for anybody who writes this inside a
  render body. This body never calls `detach()`, so the hold count grows and is never given
  back — that is one decrement we do not charge ourselves for.
  measured  95,746 ops/pass ours, 2,986,191 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 35 planned, 35/35 reached; spread 62.6%/17.5%; load 3.42 → 3.54 on 8 cores

slotOf.vs-map-get   [per relationship]   1.45x
  ours   collection.slotOf(id) — resolve a key to an identity, minting on a miss
  floor  map.get(id)
  THE FLOOR CANNOT MINT, and that is the whole difference: `slotOf` promises an identity for a
  key the population has never seen, so a caller can subscribe to a row before it arrives.
  Every key here HITS, so the minting branch never runs — what is left is the map read plus
  the closure `slotOf` allocates to pass the mint function, on every call, hit or miss.
  Direction: inflates our ratio by exactly that allocation, and it is a real cost a caller
  pays. Read this against `find.vs-map-get` directly above: the gap between the two is what
  the mint-on-miss promise costs on the hit path.
  measured  1,188,036 ops/pass ours, 2,097,152 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 24 planned, 24/24 reached; spread 23.3%/11.6%; load 3.22 → 3.37 on 8 cores

entity.vs-held-object   [per read]   1.30x
  ours   slot.entity — the row behind an identity you already hold
  floor  the object itself, held
  ONE INDIRECTION AGAINST NONE, which is the smallest thing this table can ask about and is
  close to the resolution floor — read it against the control rows before treating any part of
  it as a result. Ours answers `TPriceDataRow | undefined` because an identity may carry no
  value; the floor answers a row. This body does NOT narrow the undefined, so the branch a
  real consumer writes is charged to neither side.
  measured  14,780,126 ops/pass ours, 18,466,255 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 37 planned, 37/37 reached; spread 17.0%/19.2%; load 3.37 → 3.42 on 8 cores

find.vs-map-get   [per read]   1.19x
  ours   collection.find(id) — the identity of a key, or nothing
  floor  map.get(id)
  THE CALIBRATION ROW. `find` is one call into the identity store and one `Map.get`, so if
  this reads much above 1 the instrument is measuring call overhead rather than the library,
  and every other ratio in this table should be read with that in mind. Every key hits — a
  miss is a different path on both sides and would mix two prices. What ours hands back is an
  identity and not a row: a caller still has to read `.entity` off it, which the row below
  prices separately.
  measured  2,097,152 ops/pass ours, 2,082,050 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 21 planned, 21/21 reached; spread 19.2%/17.2%; load 2.98 → 3.22 on 8 cores

THE CONTROLS, IN FULL

control-resolution-2x   1.88x
  ours   two units of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  NOT A PRICE — an instrument check, and it is printed first because nothing below it means
  anything until it passes. The true ratio is a little UNDER 2: both sides pay the driving
  loop once, so the exact expectation is (2u + L) / (u + L) with L the loop and u one unit,
  which at sixteen dependent steps per unit puts it around 1.9. A reading below 1.5 means this
  process could not resolve a deliberate doubling, and the runner voids the report rather than
  printing prices under it.

control-scale-same-body   1.08x
  ours   one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  the same body again, over its own copy of the same state
  NOT A PRICE — the noise floor. The two sides are identical code over identical,
  separately-allocated state, so the true ratio is 1.00 exactly and every departure from it
  printed here was produced by this machine at this moment. The rule that follows is the one
  the whole table is read under: NO RATIO CLOSER TO 1.00 THAN THIS GAP IS A READING. It also
  bounds the per-row noise figure, which is the same idea measured again inside every pair —
  each pair times its floor twice, once before our side and once after, and prints how far the
  two disagreed.
