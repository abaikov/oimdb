Each row is one call this library offers, timed against the plain JavaScript a person would have
written instead of it. Not against another library — against the floor. The ratio is ours over
the floor, and it is a price tag rather than a ranking.

WHERE THE FLOOR AND OUR CALL ARE NOT DOING THE SAME WORK

There is no pair in this suite whose two sides do identical work, and a floor that does less is a
lie in our favour. Two of these are structural and apply to most rows: a Map-keyed floor pays a
hash where our identity path does not, which flatters us; and our write additionally marks a channel
and may route to indexes the floor knows nothing about, which does not. Each row says its own.

A THIRD ONE BELONGS TO THE INSTRUMENT AND APPLIES TO EVERY ROW. Both sides are driven by a loop —
an increment, a bounds check, an index wrap and one store into a sink — and that loop is charged
to the numerator and the denominator alike. Adding the same constant to both pulls a ratio toward
1.00, and it pulls hardest where the operation is cheapest, so the sub-nanosecond rows are the ones
this understates most. The wrap is a mask rather than a division for exactly that reason. Read
`find.vs-map-get` as the calibration: it is one call and one Map.get on our side against one Map.get
on the floor, so whatever it reports above 1.00 is mostly this.

subscribe.deliver-vs-direct-call   [per frame]   26.0x
  ours   update the entity, flush, and the subscription fires — per identity delivered
  floor  store the field and call the handler at once, with no flush
  The price of deferred delivery against none at all. The floor calls the handler inside the
  write, so a handler can see half of a frame's update; it is not a floor anything with
  once-per-flush delivery can reach, and `subscribe.deliver-vs-queue-at-flush` is the floor
  that is. The gap between the two pairs is what deferral itself costs by hand; the rest of
  this ratio is the identity registration, the stamp, the enlist and the frame.
  measured  766,608 ops/pass ours, 9,078,059 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 27 planned, 27/27 reached; spread 362.5%/3.8%; load 4.07 → 4.07 on 8 cores

subscribeToField.deliver-vs-filtered-queue-at-flush   [per frame]   13.5x
  ours   effectService.createEffect([column slot of 'title'], [], h) — the narrowest signal the engine has
  floor  store the field, queue the entity once when the moved name is 'title', and call h for each queued entity at flush
  The one column that moved is the one subscribed to, on both sides, and both deliver once per
  flush after every write of the frame has landed. A column of an entity is its own identity
  in ours, minted by `holdColumn`, and a write marks only the column slots that exist, so a
  `note` write does not reach a `title` subscriber at all. The mint is outside the clock (one
  per entity, at build); inside it ours pays the write path, the column mark, the enlist and
  the frame, against the floor's store, string compare, flag test and push on the write, and
  flag clear and call at flush.
  measured  187,752 ops/pass ours, 4,810,112 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 27 planned, 27/27 reached; spread 13.7%/16.3%; load 4.25 → 4.55 on 8 cores

subscribe.deliver-vs-queue-at-flush   [per frame]   8.31x
  ours   update the entity, flush, and the subscription fires — per identity delivered
  floor  store the field, queue the entity once, and call the handler for each queued entity at flush
  Both sides deliver once per flush, after every write of the frame has landed, so no handler
  sees half an update. One write per identity per frame is the worst case for us: nothing is
  saved by coalescing. Ours per delivery: the update path, the stamp, enlisting the slot's
  effects on the frame, and the frame running each body. The floor per delivery: one store, a
  flag test and a push on the write, a flag clear and the call at flush. The fixed cost of a
  frame is divided by a thousand deliveries here; `flush.empty-vs-noop` prices it on its own.
  What ours adds over the floor: the handler is registered against the identity, so a writer
  does not need to know who is listening.
  measured  708,932 ops/pass ours, 3,347,553 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 28 planned, 28/28 reached; spread 268.0%/16.7%; load 3.84 → 4.25 on 8 cores

THE CONTROLS, IN FULL

control-resolution-2x   1.91x
  ours   two units of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  NOT A PRICE — an instrument check, and it is printed first because nothing below it means
  anything until it passes. The true ratio is a little UNDER 2: both sides pay the driving
  loop once, so the exact expectation is (2u + L) / (u + L) with L the loop and u one unit,
  which at sixteen dependent steps per unit puts it around 1.9. A reading below 1.5 means this
  process could not resolve a deliberate doubling, and the runner voids the report rather than
  printing prices under it.

control-scale-same-body   1.01x
  ours   one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  the same body again, over its own copy of the same state
  NOT A PRICE — the noise floor. The two sides are identical code over identical,
  separately-allocated state, so the true ratio is 1.00 exactly and every departure from it
  printed here was produced by this machine at this moment. The rule that follows is the one
  the whole table is read under: NO RATIO CLOSER TO 1.00 THAN THIS GAP IS A READING. It also
  bounds the per-row noise figure, which is the same idea measured again inside every pair —
  each pair times its floor twice, once before our side and once after, and prints how far the
  two disagreed.
