OIMDB — THE DESIGNS WE REJECTED, MEASURED BESIDE THE ONE WE SHIPPED

Each row is one architectural decision. `ours` is the design that shipped; `floor` is the design
that was proposed and refused. Not a primitive — a candidate. The ratio is still ours over the
floor, and it is a verdict on a decision rather than a price tag.

node v24.16.0   8 cores   foreign-load gate 3.50   gc exposed

THE INSTRUMENT, BEFORE ANY VERDICT

ratio   ours  floor  noise  expected  control
2.13x  43 ns  20 ns   0.4%     ~1.9x  control-resolution-2x
0.98x  20 ns  20 ns   2.9%     1.00x  control-scale-same-body

  The resolution control saw 2.13x for a deliberate doubling of work, so this instrument resolves a 2x.
  The scale control — the same body under two names — came out 2.4% from 1.00.
  THE RULE FOR THE WHOLE TABLE: no ratio within 2.4% of 1.00 is a reading.

  AND IT BITES HARDER HERE THAN ON A PRICE TABLE. A price of 34x survives a 6% noise floor without
  a scratch — nobody rereads a 34x because the instrument wobbles by a twentieth of it. A decision
  that wins by 4% does not survive it at all: the margin IS the instrument. Such a row is not a
  narrow win and it is not a tie either — the decision is UNSETTLED, which is a different sentence
  from "the two designs are equal". Equal is a claim about the designs; unsettled is a statement
  about this machine at this moment, and a quieter one may separate them. Those rows print no
  margin, because a margin the instrument could not resolve is what gets quoted on.

HOW TO READ A ROW HERE — THE OPPOSITE OF THE PRICE TABLE

  ratio = ours / floor, where the floor is THE DESIGN WE REJECTED. So the direction is inverted
  against every other table in this suite:

    below 1.00   the shipped design wins. The margin column says by how much: it is 1 / ratio,
                 because 0.40x is the ratio and "2.5x cheaper" is what a person means by it.
    above 1.00   THE REJECTED DESIGN WON AND THE DECISION WAS WRONG. Marked `!`, and it is a
                 finding rather than an error in the harness. Several pairs in this catalogue were
                 written by their authors EXPECTING to lose — a delivery list against a scan at
                 50% dirty, coalescing at one write per node per frame, a field filter at one in
                 four skipped — because a comparison that only ever vindicates its author is a
                 comparison nobody should believe. Those rows are the most valuable in the suite.

WHERE THE TWO SIDES ARE NOT DOING THE SAME WORK

On a price table an unequal floor flatters us and that is the whole risk: a primitive doing less
than our call makes the abstraction look cheaper than it is. HERE THE RISK IS A DIFFERENT AND WORSE
ONE. A floor that is not the rejected design faithfully built means we may have refused a design we
never actually measured — and then printed a number saying we were right to. That is the failure this
catalogue exists to prevent, and it cannot be checked from a ratio.

So it is checked from the caveats, which are printed in full under every row below. Each one names
what the rejected design would have BOUGHT — the reason somebody proposed it — before it names
anything else, and states which way each inequality pushes that row's ratio. No claim is summarised
here that a row does not make for itself.

ONE THING IS STRUCTURAL AND APPLIES WHEREVER BOTH ARMS RUN THE ENGINE: the price tables put our code
against a `Map` that nothing of ours can spoil, and that is no longer true when both sides call the
same compiled function through the same inline caches. One side's shapes then reach the other's
measurement inside one process. The rows where that applies say so, and say which way it leans.

alt.remove-member.unordered-vs-ordered.10k   [per row per write]   0.14x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 7.00x cheaper
  ours     a member leaves a bucket of 10000 and joins another, membership in a Set (OIMBucketMutatorUnordered)
  floor    the same move, membership in a list kept sorted by rank (OIMBucketMutatorOrdered)
  A BUCKET OF 10000, and the size is in the id because the answer is a function of it — that
  is the entire claim under test. THE COUNT THIS PREDICTS, per member moved, on the ordered
  half: `indexOf` compares m + 1 members and `splice` moves 10000 − m − 1 of them, then the
  settle's `upperBound` spends 14 comparator calls and its splice moves about 10000 − m more,
  where m is the departing member's position. The schedule visits 16 positions evenly spaced
  over the list (0, 625, 1250, … 9375), so a pass averages them: about 5000 reference compares
  and about 10000 pointer moves per member moved. On the unordered half: one `Set.delete` and
  one `Set.add`, neither of them a function of the size, and a read that hands the set back as
  it stands. So the floor should grow in proportion to the bucket and ours should not move —
  which is the shape the published table asserts and the reason these four rows exist. ONE
  FLUSH IS SPREAD OVER 8 OPERATIONS, since a frame swaps one band in every lane at once;
  `flush.empty-vs-noop` prices a flush directly, for a reader who wants it out of the figure.
  WHAT AN OPERATION IS: one member moves from one bucket to another, through the path a
  grouped index actually takes — `collection.update` on the column the index is filed by, a
  flush that files it, and a read of every bucket in the frame. The read is not decoration: an
  index nobody reads is not an index, and on the ordered half it is where the order is
  settled. Both halves run the identical body; the only difference in the process is which
  mutator object was handed to `addIndex`. EVERY OPERATION REALLY MOVES A MEMBER, which is the
  failure this suite has already published a wrong number for: a write whose value equals the
  value the row holds moves no field, marks nothing and files nothing, and both halves then
  time an empty frame. Here the row written is in the page it is written OUT of and is given
  the group of the page it is written INTO, so the value always differs, the address always
  moves, and `moveSlotToBucket` always removes and always adds. The two rows of a swap are the
  two members of one band, one in each page of the lane, and the swap exchanges them — which
  is why this needs no `reset` at all: sizes, positions and the sweep are exactly what they
  were when the pass started, so an empty reset here is a steady state rather than a forgotten
  one. DIRECTION, four ways. (1) The write, the mark, the flush, the filing, the links and the
  read are identical on both halves and inside both numbers, which pulls the RATIO toward 1.00
  — so the ratio is a lower bound and the two printed nanosecond figures, whose difference is
  the container difference, are the numbers to read against the published table. (2) The
  ordered half is charged for keeping the ARRIVAL in order as well as for the removal — a
  binary search and one more splice — where the published table claims to price removal alone,
  so the ordered figure here is larger than that column by roughly one memmove of the bucket.
  (3) Both lists are walked every frame and stay in cache; a real index of this size read once
  a frame would be colder, which flatters the ordered half most at the largest size. (4) The
  unordered half is not flat for free either: a `Set` added to and deleted from fills its
  entry table with tombstones and is rehashed when it fills, which is one pass over the set
  every capacity-many insertions — an amortised handful of element copies per operation, and
  the one way this side can carry the bucket size at all. A PASS IS WHOLE FRAMES, because half
  a swap would leave both members of a band in one page and every position after it would be a
  lie; so a pass may overshoot the operation count the prober asked for by fewer operations
  than there are pages, out of the tens of thousands it asks for.
  measured  32,768 ops/pass ours, 4,096 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 20 planned, 20/20 reached; spread 369.7%/127.9%; load 3.85 → 4.52 on 8 cores

THE CONTROLS, IN FULL

control-resolution-2x   2.13x
  ours   two units of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  NOT A PRICE — an instrument check, and it is printed first because nothing below it means
  anything until it passes. The true ratio is a little UNDER 2: both sides pay the driving
  loop once, so the exact expectation is (2u + L) / (u + L) with L the loop and u one unit,
  which at sixteen dependent steps per unit puts it around 1.9. A reading below 1.5 means this
  process could not resolve a deliberate doubling, and the runner voids the report rather than
  printing prices under it.

control-scale-same-body   0.98x
  ours   one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  the same body again, over its own copy of the same state
  NOT A PRICE — the noise floor. The two sides are identical code over identical,
  separately-allocated state, so the true ratio is 1.00 exactly and every departure from it
  printed here was produced by this machine at this moment. The rule that follows is the one
  the whole table is read under: NO RATIO CLOSER TO 1.00 THAN THIS GAP IS A READING. It also
  bounds the per-row noise figure, which is the same idea measured again inside every pair —
  each pair times its floor twice, once before our side and once after, and prints how far the
  two disagreed.
