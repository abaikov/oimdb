  THE RULE FOR THE WHOLE TABLE: no ratio within 1.1% of 1.00 is a reading.

  AND IT BITES HARDER HERE THAN ON A PRICE TABLE. A price of 34x survives a 6% noise floor without
  a scratch — nobody rereads a 34x because the instrument wobbles by a twentieth of it. A decision
  that wins by 4% does not survive it at all: the margin IS the instrument. Such a row is not a
  narrow win and it is not a tie either — the decision is UNSETTLED, which is a different sentence
  from "the two designs are equal". Equal is a claim about the designs; unsettled is a statement
  about this machine at this moment, and a quieter one may separate them. Those rows print no
  margin, because a margin the instrument could not resolve is what gets quoted on.

HOW TO READ A ROW HERE — THE OPPOSITE OF THE PRICE TABLE

  ratio = ours / floor, where the floor is THE DESIGN WE REJECTED. So the direction is inverted
  against every other table in this suite:

    below 1.00   the shipped design wins. The margin column says by how much: it is 1 / ratio,
                 because 0.40x is the ratio and "2.5x cheaper" is what a person means by it.
    above 1.00   THE REJECTED DESIGN WON AND THE DECISION WAS WRONG. Marked `!`, and it is a
                 finding rather than an error in the harness. Several pairs in this catalogue were
                 written by their authors EXPECTING to lose — a delivery list against a scan at
                 50% dirty, coalescing at one write per node per frame, a field filter at one in
                 four skipped — because a comparison that only ever vindicates its author is a
                 comparison nobody should believe. Those rows are the most valuable in the suite.

WHERE THE TWO SIDES ARE NOT DOING THE SAME WORK

On a price table an unequal floor flatters us and that is the whole risk: a primitive doing less
than our call makes the abstraction look cheaper than it is. HERE THE RISK IS A DIFFERENT AND WORSE
ONE. A floor that is not the rejected design faithfully built means we may have refused a design we
never actually measured — and then printed a number saying we were right to. That is the failure this
catalogue exists to prevent, and it cannot be checked from a ratio.

So it is checked from the caveats, which are printed in full under every row below. Each one names
what the rejected design would have BOUGHT — the reason somebody proposed it — before it names
anything else, and states which way each inequality pushes that row's ratio. No claim is summarised
here that a row does not make for itself.

ONE THING IS STRUCTURAL AND APPLIES WHEREVER BOTH ARMS RUN THE ENGINE: the price tables put our code
against a `Map` that nothing of ours can spoil, and that is no longer true when both sides call the
same compiled function through the same inline caches. One side's shapes then reach the other's
measurement inside one process. The rows where that applies say so, and say which way it leans.

alt.state-on-the-carrier-vs-a-keyed-map   [per read]   0.13x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 7.65x cheaper
  ours     slot.cache[column] — per-slot state read off the identity you already hold
  floor    REJECTED: stateByKey.get(row.id) — a Map keyed by the primary key
  WHAT THE REJECTED DESIGN WOULD HAVE BOUGHT: the state is reachable WITHOUT holding the
  identity. A key out of a URL, a server payload or a dispatched action reaches it directly,
  nothing has to have resolved a slot first, and the state survives the slot entirely — which
  is exactly what you want for something keyed by an id that has not arrived yet. The cell
  cannot do any of that, and this row is the price of the convenience rather than a verdict on
  it. WHERE THE TWO ARE NOT EQUAL. Both sides do one array load and then one field load —
  `slot.cache` against `row.id` — and diverge only after it, which is what makes this the
  clean statement of the identity principle. Against us: our undefined check, again charged to
  nobody else. In the floor's favour, and this is the big one: V8 memoises a string's hash in
  the string header on first use, and these are the SAME string objects on every pass of every
  sample, so the floor never once pays to hash a fresh key — which is precisely what a real
  application does when the key came out of a JSON parse or a template literal. Direction:
  understates the gap, and by more than any other item in this file. Read this row beside
  `find.vs-map-get`: that one prices resolving a key to an identity ONCE, this one prices the
  alternative to having done so, per read, for ever.
  measured  18,270,792 ops/pass ours, 2,097,152 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 21 planned, 21/21 reached; spread 4.4%/23.4%; load 3.34 → 3.34 on 8 cores

alt.state-on-the-carrier-vs-a-strong-side-map   [per read]   0.16x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 6.40x cheaper
  ours     slot.cache[column] — per-slot state read off the identity you already hold
  floor    REJECTED: stateBySlot.get(slot) — a plain Map keyed by the identity
  THE CONTROL ON THE ROW ABOVE, and the reason it exists is that nobody would ship this one. A
  non-weak `Map` keyed by the slot retains every identity it has ever been shown for the life
  of the process, which is the leak the WeakMap exists to prevent — so what this design would
  have bought is nothing a person wants, and it is here as an instrument rather than as a
  candidate. What it IS, is the fastest object-keyed side map V8 offers. So: the distance
  between this ratio and the WeakMap ratio is what WEAKNESS costs, and this ratio alone is
  what side-map ADDRESSING costs with the lifetime question set aside entirely. If our side
  does not beat this floor, the addressing argument has not been made and the WeakMap row was
  measuring the weak references rather than the design. Every inequality listed on the row
  above applies here unchanged except its last point: the identical array load in both bodies,
  the undefined check charged only to us, the all-hit population, and the fact that our cost
  is flat in the population size while a hash table's is not.
  measured  18,999,096 ops/pass ours, 2,097,152 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 23 planned, 23/23 reached; spread 8.1%/9.9%; load 3.20 → 3.34 on 8 cores

alt.state-on-the-carrier-vs-a-side-map   [per read]   0.22x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 4.63x cheaper
  ours     slot.cache[column] — per-slot state read off the identity you already hold
  floor    REJECTED: stateBySlot.get(slot) — a WeakMap keyed by the identity
  WHAT THE REJECTED DESIGN WOULD HAVE BOUGHT, first, because it is the reason somebody
  proposed it: a WeakMap keyed by the identity keeps the state with WHOEVER MADE IT. Drop the
  map and the entire cache goes without touching a single slot; two consumers can never land
  on the same storage; and an entry vanishes on its own once nothing else holds the slot, so a
  churning population leaves nothing behind. A cell on the carrier has none of that — it
  outlives the consumer that wrote it, two consumers naming one column overwrite each other,
  and only `forget` or the slot's own death removes it. This row prices the ONE property the
  carrier has, addressing, and nothing else; the lifetime question is not a ratio and is not
  settled here. WHERE THE TWO ARE NOT EQUAL. (1) Ours is the shipped `cache` cell — a field
  load, an undefined check and an element load — which is the MORE expensive of the engine's
  two per-carrier shapes: a declared field such as `slot.linkCount` is one load and no check,
  so the engine's own per-carrier reads are cheaper than what is priced here as ours. Pushes
  the ratio up, against us. (2) Both sides walk the same slot array with the same mask, so an
  identical array load sits in the numerator and the denominator, and adding the same constant
  to both pulls a ratio below 1 toward 1 — it understates the gap. (3) Every cell is filled
  and every key hits, so neither side takes a miss branch. (4) The WeakMap holds 8192 live
  entries. Our cost does not move with population size and a hash table's does, so this ratio
  is not a constant: a smaller population flatters the floor, a larger one flatters us. (5)
  THIS IS NOT THE FASTEST SIDE MAP THERE IS — a non-weak `Map` keyed by the slot skips the
  weak-reference bookkeeping, and it is priced separately below precisely so this row cannot
  be accused of having picked the floor that loses.
  measured  18,876,820 ops/pass ours, 2,097,152 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 26 planned, 26/26 reached; spread 8.8%/13.3%; load 3.39 → 3.39 on 8 cores

alt.entity-cache-cell-vs-weakmap   [per read]   0.30x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 3.36x cheaper
  ours     cellCache.get(slot) — OIMEntityCacheCellDriven, as shipped
  floor    REJECTED: weakCache.get(slot) — OIMEntityCacheWeakDriven, as shipped
  THE CHEAPEST ROW IN THIS FILE TO WRITE HONESTLY, because both sides are classes this package
  already ships and neither had to be reconstructed from a comment. Note what that makes it:
  the WeakMap side is NOT a rejected design at all — both are exported and documented, and the
  choice between them is a consumer's. What was rejected is only the claim that they cost the
  same. WHAT THE WEAK ONE BUYS is written on its own class and is real: the cache belongs to
  whoever made it and dies with them, two consumers can never collide, and entries vanish as
  the population churns — where a cell outlives its writer and two consumers naming one column
  overwrite each other. WHERE THE TWO ARE NOT EQUAL. Each side is one method call on a
  receiver of exactly one class, from its own call site, so both are monomorphic — which is
  also what a consumer holding one of the two gets, so this is not an artificial advantage for
  either. The call overhead is on both sides and pulls the ratio toward 1, understating
  whatever gap exists. Ours additionally loads `this.column` and takes the undefined check on
  `slot.cache`; the floor loads `this.byRow`. Every slot is populated in both, so no miss
  branch runs on either side. Population-size direction as elsewhere: our cost is flat in it,
  a WeakMap's is not. AND THE FIGURE THIS REPLACES: two source files, the package README,
  `llms.txt` and two docs pages all state 3.677 ns against 8.887 on a 0.117 ns floor. Nothing
  here subtracts a floor from either side — the two absolute nanosecond figures the report
  prints are what the process measured, and the ratio is theirs.
  measured  13,109,114 ops/pass ours, 2,097,152 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 26 planned, 26/26 reached; spread 17.6%/6.6%; load 3.16 → 3.16 on 8 cores

alt.one-class-vs-four-subclasses   [per row per write]   0.88x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 1.13x cheaper
  ours     fill four populations of ONE collection class — 8192 upserts
  floor    REJECTED: fill four populations of FOUR empty subclasses — 8192 upserts
  WHAT THE REJECTED DESIGN WOULD HAVE BOUGHT: `groupBy`, `groupByEach`, `groupByCompositeKey`
  and `all` as METHODS. Discoverable by typing a dot on the population, typed to it without a
  generic argument, and with no free function for a caller to find and import. That is the DX
  the four free functions gave up, and it is not a small thing to give up. Note that the
  free-function decision ALSO rests on a bundling argument — a class body cannot be partially
  tree-shaken, so a method that constructs its strategies inline pins them into every consumer
  — which this row does not measure and which does not depend on what it reports. THE GEOMETRY
  IS HELD STILL, and that is what makes this pair different from the experiment behind the
  +76% nobody can find. Both sides run FOUR populations of 2048 rows: same total rows, same
  total writes, same number of populations, same key selector object, same database options,
  same loop. One population of N against four of N/4 would have been a different experiment,
  whose split moves the number on its own — so the split is on both sides and cancels, and
  what is left between them is how many hidden classes the engine sees. The subclasses are
  EMPTY because the claim says the price is the shape and not the methods; bodies would
  measure the bodies too. THIS DOES NOT REPRODUCE THE ORIGINAL EXPERIMENT and cannot: nothing
  records what it did. `website/docs/performance/index.md` already carries the verdict that
  +76% is unsupported and that the +14% offered to correct it is too. This row replaces both
  rather than adjudicating between them. THIS PAIR DECLARES `isolate`, AND IT IS THE REASON
  THAT CAPABILITY EXISTS. Both sides call the SAME compiled `OIMCollection` methods, whose
  inline caches belong to their call sites and not to the receiver. Measured the ordinary way
  — both sides built and timed in one process — those caches would have seen all five maps by
  the time our side ran, the base class and the four subclasses, and our side would be
  measured through caches the rejected design spoiled. That does not merely bias the number,
  it makes it UNREADABLE: a ratio at 1.00 would be equally consistent with "the shape costs
  nothing" and with "the other arm spoiled the caches ours ran through". So each side is
  probed and timed in a process that has never constructed the other, five processes in all,
  and `build` is told which side is wanted — declining to TIME the other design is not enough,
  because merely FILLING its populations teaches V8 its shape. What remains charged honestly
  is the timed body's own call site: ours dispatches to one map there and the floor to four,
  which is exactly what a consumer of four subclasses would have had. The residual cost is
  that `noise` now includes process-to-process variation, which is the honest thing for it to
  include here. Each side gets its OWN database, so no flush ever walks the other side's
  frame, and each pass ends with exactly one flush per 8192 rows. Both sides abandon their
  four populations between passes and the collection runs outside the clock.
  measured  8,192 ops/pass ours, 8,192 floor; 15 samples; warm-up 628 planned, 628/628 reached; spread 20.5%/10.9%; load 3.06 → 3.06 on 8 cores

THE CONTROLS, IN FULL

control-resolution-2x   1.94x
  ours   two units of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  NOT A PRICE — an instrument check, and it is printed first because nothing below it means
  anything until it passes. The true ratio is a little UNDER 2: both sides pay the driving
  loop once, so the exact expectation is (2u + L) / (u + L) with L the loop and u one unit,
  which at sixteen dependent steps per unit puts it around 1.9. A reading below 1.5 means this
  process could not resolve a deliberate doubling, and the runner voids the report rather than
  printing prices under it.

control-scale-same-body   0.99x
  ours   one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  the same body again, over its own copy of the same state
  NOT A PRICE — the noise floor. The two sides are identical code over identical,
  separately-allocated state, so the true ratio is 1.00 exactly and every departure from it
  printed here was produced by this machine at this moment. The rule that follows is the one
  the whole table is read under: NO RATIO CLOSER TO 1.00 THAN THIS GAP IS A READING. It also
  bounds the per-row noise figure, which is the same idea measured again inside every pair —
  each pair times its floor twice, once before our side and once after, and prints how far the
  two disagreed.
