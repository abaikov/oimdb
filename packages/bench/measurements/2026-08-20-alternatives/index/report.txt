Each row is one architectural decision. `ours` is the design that shipped; `floor` is the design
that was proposed and refused. Not a primitive — a candidate. The ratio is still ours over the
floor, and it is a verdict on a decision rather than a price tag.

THE INSTRUMENT, BEFORE ANY VERDICT

ratio   ours  floor  noise  expected  control
1.93x  37 ns  19 ns   1.5%     ~1.9x  control-resolution-2x
1.00x  19 ns  19 ns   0.4%     1.00x  control-scale-same-body

  The resolution control saw 1.93x for a deliberate doubling of work, so this instrument resolves a 2x.
  The scale control — the same body under two names — came out 0.2% from 1.00.
  THE RULE FOR THE WHOLE TABLE: no ratio within 0.2% of 1.00 is a reading.

  AND IT BITES HARDER HERE THAN ON A PRICE TABLE. A price of 34x survives a 6% noise floor without
  a scratch — nobody rereads a 34x because the instrument wobbles by a twentieth of it. A decision
  that wins by 4% does not survive it at all: the margin IS the instrument. Such a row is not a
  narrow win and it is not a tie either — the decision is UNSETTLED, which is a different sentence
  from "the two designs are equal". Equal is a claim about the designs; unsettled is a statement
  about this machine at this moment, and a quieter one may separate them. Those rows print no
  margin, because a margin the instrument could not resolve is what gets quoted on.

HOW TO READ A ROW HERE — THE OPPOSITE OF THE PRICE TABLE

  ratio = ours / floor, where the floor is THE DESIGN WE REJECTED. So the direction is inverted
  against every other table in this suite:

    below 1.00   the shipped design wins. The margin column says by how much: it is 1 / ratio,
                 because 0.40x is the ratio and "2.5x cheaper" is what a person means by it.
    above 1.00   THE REJECTED DESIGN WON AND THE DECISION WAS WRONG. Marked `!`, and it is a
                 finding rather than an error in the harness. Several pairs in this catalogue were
                 written by their authors EXPECTING to lose — a delivery list against a scan at
                 50% dirty, coalescing at one write per node per frame, a field filter at one in
                 four skipped — because a comparison that only ever vindicates its author is a
                 comparison nobody should believe. Those rows are the most valuable in the suite.

WHERE THE TWO SIDES ARE NOT DOING THE SAME WORK

On a price table an unequal floor flatters us and that is the whole risk: a primitive doing less
than our call makes the abstraction look cheaper than it is. HERE THE RISK IS A DIFFERENT AND WORSE
ONE. A floor that is not the rejected design faithfully built means we may have refused a design we
never actually measured — and then printed a number saying we were right to. That is the failure this
catalogue exists to prevent, and it cannot be checked from a ratio.

So it is checked from the caveats, which are printed in full under every row below. Each one names
what the rejected design would have BOUGHT — the reason somebody proposed it — before it names
anything else, and states which way each inequality pushes that row's ratio. No claim is summarised
here that a row does not make for itself.

ONE THING IS STRUCTURAL AND APPLIES WHEREVER BOTH ARMS RUN THE ENGINE: the price tables put our code
against a `Map` that nothing of ours can spoil, and that is no longer true when both sides call the
same compiled function through the same inline caches. One side's shapes then reach the other's
measurement inside one process. The rows where that applies say so, and say which way it leans.

alt.reposition-one-vs-full-resort.1k   [per frame]   0.00x
  verdict  UNSETTLED — the difference is inside this run's own error. Neither design is shown to win.
  ours     one member of a bucket of 1000 is written and the bucket is read — settled by reposition
  floor    the same write and the same read, settled by sorting the whole bucket
  A BUCKET OF 1000, and the size is in the id because the answer depends on it: the repair is
  one scan plus two splices plus a binary search where the sort is a pass over everything, so
  a single size would be a pair that picked its own answer. Every operation is one row's sort
  column written, one frame closed, and one bucket read — the read is where an order is
  settled, on both designs. WHAT THE FULL RE-SORT BUYS is everything `membersInOrder` is: the
  budget arithmetic that decides when repairing one member at a time has stopped being cheaper
  than sorting, the reproduction of `Array.prototype.sort`'s stable tie-breaking so a member
  reported moved whose key did not move goes back exactly where it sat, the two rescue paths
  that put movers back when the caller's comparator throws with them spliced out of the list,
  and the walk that spots a bulk load which arrived already in order. Five hundred lines
  against three, and the three cannot be got wrong. DIRECTION, three ways, and two of them are
  ours: (1) the write, the frame and the read call are IDENTICAL on both halves and are inside
  both numbers, which pulls this ratio toward 1.00 — the settle-only difference is larger than
  this row shows, and that is most of the distance between this row and the 13x/22x/26x our
  own notes remember for the settle alone; (2) the floor keeps neither a newcomers list nor a
  movers list, so the write-side bookkeeping our design pays for is charged to us and to
  nothing on the other side; (3) against those, the floor is NOT charged for the one thing a
  sort cannot answer — whether the order actually CHANGED. Ours computes it as a by-product (a
  mover's landing against where it sat) and the engine wakes a subscriber only when it says
  yes; the floor would need a copy of the list and a walk of it, and since nothing here
  subscribes to these buckets it never pays for one. That flatters the floor.
  measured  760,434 ops/pass ours, 1,024 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 28 planned, 28/28 reached; spread 10.3%/33.1%; load 3.35 → 3.96 on 8 cores

alt.key-on-the-slot-vs-two-set-diff   [per row per write]   0.37x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 2.67x cheaper
  ours     the bucket is on the slot and carries its key — one === and no lookup
  floor    recompute, recall from a Map, and diff the two through a pair of Sets
  THE DESIGN THIS ONE REPLACED, and the reason the fix was to make the eager recompute cheap
  rather than to make it lazy: what cost 174 ns was not "index work", it was two allocations
  and a hash on a path that had already decided nothing needed to move. The diff BUYS
  cardinality-independence — one body serves one address and fifty, so the two membership
  sources this package ships would have been one class. EVERY OPERATION IS ONE ROW WRITTEN AND
  ONE ROW RE-FILED, and the address never moves: the selector reads two columns, the write
  moves the one that does not decide the address, and the index recomputes the group the row
  already sits in. That is the ordinary case for any address derived from more than one
  column, and it is where the three designs differ. DIRECTION: the write, the mark, the delta
  and the frame are identical on both halves and are inside both numbers, so this ratio is
  pulled hard toward 1.00 — the per-filing difference our own notes remember (174.1 against
  34.8 against 7.5 ns) was measured on the filing alone and is necessarily wider than what
  this row prints. Two things flatter the FLOOR and are stated so nobody has to find them: it
  is handed the row's index cell to find the bucket it reports on, which its own design would
  have resolved with another lookup; and the `Map` of one entry per row that it keeps alive —
  memory our design does not spend at all, because the answer rides on the row — is in neither
  number.
  measured  668,350 ops/pass ours, 310,534 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 22 planned, 22/22 reached; spread 20.3%/15.1%; load 3.74 → 4.08 on 8 cores

alt.linear-scan-vs-set-diff.2   [per row per write]   0.59x
  verdict  the shipped design wins — 1.69x cheaper
  ours     re-file a row under 2 addresses — two linear scans through the store's own comparison
  floor    the same re-file, diffed through a Set of the 2 keys
  2 ADDRESSES PER ROW. Ours is O(A²) word comparisons and no allocation; the floor is O(A)
  with two `Set`s allocated per write. THE CROSSOVER IS THE CLAIM this family exists to check,
  and it is the one thing the shared cost cannot distort: the write, the frame and the
  selector are identical on both halves, so they add the same constant to both numbers — which
  pulls each RATIO toward 1.00 but leaves the size at which the two designs are equal exactly
  where it was. Read the family, not the row. WHAT THE SET BUYS is that it does not degrade:
  at some size the scans lose and keep losing. WHAT IT GIVES UP is not nanoseconds — the scans
  ask the STORE whether a bucket is addressed by a key, so an index filed by a chain of
  identities is served by the shipped design and cannot be served by this floor at all, which
  is why this floor is writable only over a keyed store. Every timed operation leaves the
  address list unchanged, which is the steady state both designs are written for; the floor
  delegates the leaves and the joins of a CHANGED list to the shipped code, and no timed
  operation takes that branch.
  measured  495,360 ops/pass ours, 159,785 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 26 planned, 26/26 reached; spread 27.5%/28.9%; load 4.16 → 5.74 on 8 cores

THE CONTROLS, IN FULL

control-resolution-2x   1.93x
  ours   two units of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  NOT A PRICE — an instrument check, and it is printed first because nothing below it means
  anything until it passes. The true ratio is a little UNDER 2: both sides pay the driving
  loop once, so the exact expectation is (2u + L) / (u + L) with L the loop and u one unit,
  which at sixteen dependent steps per unit puts it around 1.9. A reading below 1.5 means this
  process could not resolve a deliberate doubling, and the runner voids the report rather than
  printing prices under it.

control-scale-same-body   1.00x
  ours   one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  the same body again, over its own copy of the same state
  NOT A PRICE — the noise floor. The two sides are identical code over identical,
  separately-allocated state, so the true ratio is 1.00 exactly and every departure from it
  printed here was produced by this machine at this moment. The rule that follows is the one
  the whole table is read under: NO RATIO CLOSER TO 1.00 THAN THIS GAP IS A READING. It also
  bounds the per-row noise figure, which is the same idea measured again inside every pair —
  each pair times its floor twice, once before our side and once after, and prints how far the
  two disagreed.
