WHERE THE FLOOR AND OUR CALL ARE NOT DOING THE SAME WORK

There is no pair in this suite whose two sides do identical work, and a floor that does less is a
lie in our favour. Two of these are structural and apply to most rows: a Map-keyed floor pays a
hash where our identity path does not, which flatters us; and our write additionally marks a channel
and may route to indexes the floor knows nothing about, which does not. Each row says its own.

A THIRD ONE BELONGS TO THE INSTRUMENT AND APPLIES TO EVERY ROW. Both sides are driven by a loop —
an increment, a bounds check, an index wrap and one store into a sink — and that loop is charged
to the numerator and the denominator alike. Adding the same constant to both pulls a ratio toward
1.00, and it pulls hardest where the operation is cheapest, so the sub-nanosecond rows are the ones
this understates most. The wrap is a mask rather than a division for exactly that reason. Read
`find.vs-map-get` as the calibration: it is one call and one Map.get on our side against one Map.get
on the floor, so whatever it reports above 1.00 is mostly this.

sweep.grouped-column.manual-set-members   [per write]   166x
  ours   entityContentService.updateEntity(slot, { group }), bucketContentService.writeBucketMemberSlots on both buckets, then bucketContentService.getBucketMemberSlots(bucket)
  floor  move the row between two Sets, write the column, iterate one Set
  THE ARM IS THE WHOLE-BUCKET DOOR, IN PLACE. THE MEMBERSHIP IS THE CALLER'S ON THIS ARM: the
  bucket collection is not an index and hears no write, so the move is made in the same
  statement sequence as the write, inside the timed body. WHAT THE ARM PAYS THAT THE FLOOR
  DOES NOT: `updateEntity` walks the patch, gates the column, builds the moved-column list and
  marks the row on the channel; then the move marks both buckets and keeps the slot's hold
  count. THE ADDRESS IS RESOLVED THE SAME WAY ON BOTH SIDES: one `getSlotByKey` per end of the
  move, which is the hash the floor pays as `byGroup.get(next)`. THE OLD GROUP IS READ OFF THE
  STORED ROW BEFORE THE WRITE LANDS, which a caller holding the slot can always do.
  `writeBucketMemberSlots` is handed each bucket's new membership, asks the bucket service
  whether the bucket already holds exactly those slots, and — since it does not — replaces the
  set and reports who arrived and who left. Moving one row is therefore TWO FULL BUCKET
  REWRITES, plus the caller's own `indexOf` and `splice` on the array it keeps beside them, on
  groups of a hundred and twenty-eight. THIS IS THE DOOR A RE-HYDRATED SERVER LIST USES and
  pricing it on a one-row move is pricing it on its worst task; the distance between it and
  the other arms is what the finer doors are worth. THE FLOOR IS THE SAME CODE FOR EVERY ARM
  OF THIS TASK, which is the property that makes the arms comparable at all. It moves the row
  between two `Set`s — the container our unordered bucket holds its members in, so this is the
  structural equal and not a cheaper shape — writes the column, and iterates one group. WHAT
  IT DOES LESS OF, and every item pushes our ratio UP: it keeps no identity that outlives the
  value, so nothing can be subscribed to a row or to a group; it stamps no clock; it records
  no moved columns; it keeps no hold count, so a group cannot be resolved by address and
  handed to a consumer; and its groups can be read by iterating them and by nothing else. WHAT
  IS ON BOTH SIDES AND CANCELS: the read group is held from outside the clock on both, and the
  read visits the same members on both. WHERE THE FLOOR IS CHARGED MORE THAN IT SHOULD BE,
  stated because it flatters us: it hashes `row.group` to find the departure set, where the
  index arm reads the previous key off the slot's own index-key cell.
  measured  2,048 ops/pass ours, 509,250 floor; 9 samples; warm-up 23 planned, 23/23 reached; spread 6.3%/60.0%; load 4.92 → 4.92 on 8 cores

upsert.whole-row-vs-five-stores   [per write]   32.5x
  ours   collection.upsertEntity(row) — a key that already carries a value
  floor  copy five columns onto the stored object, one store each
  THE FLOOR CANNOT EXPRESS ONE HALF OF WHAT UPSERT PROMISES. An upsert of an existing key also
  REMOVES the columns the new value does not carry, which means walking the stored row to find
  them; here both rows carry all five columns, so that walk finds nothing and the floor's five
  stores are the same net effect. Ours additionally gates each column on the default equality,
  so only the column that actually moved is written and reported, where the floor writes all
  five every time. THAT DEFAULT IS SAMEVALUEZERO AND WAS AN INLINE `===` WHEN THIS ROW WAS
  RECORDED: the four columns this fixture does not move settle on the first clause and cost
  what they cost before, and the one that moves carries one comparison this figure never saw.
  Direction: mixed — the gate saves us four stores and costs us five comparisons plus a
  moved-list allocation, and the marking and routing are ours alone. Read it beside
  update.column: the difference between the two is what sending a whole row costs over sending
  the column that changed. THE STRUCTURAL CAVEAT AT THE HEAD OF THIS REPORT RUNS BACKWARDS ON
  THIS ROW AND ON NO OTHER WRITE ROW. It says a Map-keyed floor pays a hash our identity path
  does not, which flatters us. Here it is the reverse: the floor is handed `held[i]` by its
  index and never looks anything up, while ours is handed a ROW and must resolve its key —
  `keyOf` and then the store lookup inside `writeContentByKey`, a `Map.get` over eight
  thousand string keys. That lookup is inside our number and outside the floor's, so this row
  overstates what the design costs a caller who already holds the identity.
  `upsert.whole-row-vs-map-get-and-five-stores` is the same measurement with the lookup on
  both sides; the difference between the two rows is the hash, and neither alone is the price
  of an upsert.

control-resolution-2x   1.94x
  ours   two units of arithmetic per iteration
  floor  one unit of arithmetic per iteration
  NOT A PRICE — an instrument check, and it is printed first because nothing below it means
  anything until it passes. The true ratio is a little UNDER 2: both sides pay the driving
  loop once, so the exact expectation is (2u + L) / (u + L) with L the loop and u one unit,
  which at sixteen dependent steps per unit puts it around 1.9. A reading below 1.5 means this
  process could not resolve a deliberate doubling, and the runner voids the report rather than
  printing prices under it.
